**My Approach, Step by Step**

**1**. **How I would select the category**

I’d choose the category before choosing products, using clear criteria, so the decision is principled and not random.

**Criterion**        /       **What I’m looking for**

1. Fit with During Days	:  Home, furniture, garden and storage, where the business already has knowledge.
2. Market growth	    :      Rising or stable demand (Google Trends, Australia Post and ABS data).
3. Margin potential	    :  Enough gap between sourcing cost and typical retail price.
4. Practicality	        :  Not too fragile, heavy or regulated; manageable shipping and returns.
5. Competition gap	    :      Not completely dominated by one giant retailer.

I’d list 4-5 candidate categories (for example storage and organisation, outdoor living, home office, pet furniture), score each 1-5 on the criteria above, and pick the highest total. Seasonality matters too: it’s currently November, heading into an Australian summer, so I’d check whether outdoor categories are peaking or about to fade.

**2**. **Where I would research**

I’d use a mix of source types so no single bias dominates:

**Purpose**	        /        **Sources**

1. Demand and trends	     : Google Trends (Australia), Amazon Australia Best Sellers, eBay Australia sold listings.
2. Market size and growth	 : Australia Post eCommerce Report, ABS retail trade data.
3. Competitors and prices	 : Kmart, Big W, Bunnings, Temple & Webster, Kogan, PriceSpy Australia.
4. Customer voice	         : Product reviews, Reddit (e.g. r/australia), Facebook groups, Gumtree.
5. Sourcing cost	         : Supplier marketplaces and quotes (an estimate only until verified).

Catch.com.au is excluded, as Wesfarmers has wound it down. I’d also set up one tracking sheet immediately and log every source and date as I go.

**3**. **How I would identify potential products**

1. Scan best-seller and “new arrivals” pages in the chosen category on the main marketplaces and retailers.

2. Look for gaps: products with strong reviews but few sellers, frequent complaints I could solve (“too small”, “poor quality”), or items trending on Google Trends with limited Australian supply.

3. Build a longlist of 5-8 products.

4. Apply quick filters: drop anything bulky and expensive to ship, legally restricted, or too trademarked or branded to copy.

5. Score the survivors on demand, competition gap, margin potential and practicality, then pick one lead product, with one backup.


**4**. **How I would research competitors**
For the lead product, I’d identify the 4-5 main sellers, a mix of large retailers and marketplace sellers. For each competitor I’d record

- Product name, URL, price, any discount

- Shipping cost and delivery time

- Ratings and review count (a demand signal)

- Seller type (retailer vs. third-party) and stock status

- What they do well and where customer reviews show weaknesses

Reading the one- and two-star reviews is especially valuable, since that’s where unmet needs and differentiation ideas appear.

**5**. **How I would compare prices**
I’d use the principle from Q10: compare total landed price, not headline price.


- Same product, same specification, same condition

- Same delivery postcode, with shipping included

- Regular vs. sale price recorded separately, with only deals available to normal customers counted

- All prices collected in the same session, with the date recorded

- Price history checked on PriceSpy to see whether a “sale” is real

Output: the lowest, average, and highest market price (=MIN, =AVERAGE, =MAX), and the “typical” range where most competitors sit. That range anchors my suggested selling price.

**6**. **How I would estimate demand**

No single number gives demand, so I’d triangulate with several signals:

**Signal**                     /          **What it tells me**

Google Trends (Australia, 12 months)  : 	Direction and seasonality of interest.
Marketplace best-seller ranks	      :     Current sales momentum.
Review counts and review growth	      : Real purchase volume (a proxy, since most buyers don’t review).
Number of sold listings on eBay	      : Actual transaction activity.
Category growth (Australia Post, ABS) : Whether the wider market is expanding.


Result: a rating of High, Medium or Low demand with the evidence behind it. I’d be clear that this is an estimate, not actual sales. 

**7**. **How I would assess competition**

**Factor**                       /          **Question** 

Number of sellers	                  : How crowded is it?
Market concentration	              : Is one big player dominant?
Price pressure	                      : Are prices already being cut hard?
Differentiation room	              : Can we offer something better (bundle, quality, service)?
Barriers	                          : Brand strength, shipping advantages, compliance needs

Result: High, Medium or Low competition, with a short explanation. A good opportunity usually combines solid demand with a competition gap, not just high demand.



**8**. **How I would assess profitability**

Gross profit      =  Selling price − Product cost.
Net profit        =  Gross profit − (Payment fee + Shipping + Marketing + Returns allowance).
Net margin        =  Net profit ÷ Selling price.
Break-even price  = (Cost + Shipping + Marketing) ÷ (1 − payment fee %).

Illustrative example (hypothetical numbers): if a product costs $60, sells for $119, with a 3% payment fee ($3.57), $8 shipping and $10 marketing, then net profit = 119 − 60 − 21.57 = $37.43, a 31.5% net margin. I’d run low, base and high scenarios (e.g. cost +10%, price −10%) to show how sensitive the result is, and set a minimum acceptable net margin (for example 15-20%) as the go or no-go threshold.

Since the real cost isn’t confirmed, I’d flag it as the biggest assumption and verify it with supplier quotes before launch.

**9**. **What data I would collect**

**Category**	      /        **Data points**

Product	             : Name, category, specs, variants, supplier or source.
Prices	             : Retail price, sale price, shipping, total landed price, date checked.
Competitors	         : Seller, URL, rating, review count, stock, delivery time.
Demand	             : Google Trends link or screenshot, best-seller rank, review volume, sold listings.
Costs	             : Estimated supplier cost, shipping, payment fee, marketing, returns allowance.
Risks	             : Seasonality, compliance, trademark, fragility, return rate.
Sources       	     : Every URL with the date accessed.

All of it goes into one tracking sheet, and every claim in my recommendation links back to a source.

**10**. **How I would present my final recommendation**

1. Recommendation: the product, in one line, with a clear Go, Conditional Go or No-go
2. Why: 3 bullets (demand, competition gap, profit)
3. Key numbers: price range, suggested selling price, estimated net margin, break-even
4. Evidence: a small competitor table and a demand summary with source links
5. Risks and assumptions: estimated cost, seasonality, shipping, returns
6. Next steps: verify supplier cost, order samples, run a small test batch, set a review date
